The walk is the highlight of most dogs' day. The right gear won't train your dog for you, but it makes walks safer, more comfortable and a lot less stressful for both ends of the lead. You don't need a cupboard full of kit: a few well-chosen pieces, fitted properly, cover almost everything.

## Collar or harness?

Most dogs benefit from having both.

- **A collar** carries the ID tag and is handy for quick moments, such as clipping on the lead at the door. A flat, adjustable webbing collar like our Everyday Webbing Collar is comfortable for everyday wear.
- **A harness** spreads pressure across the chest instead of the neck. It's the better choice for dogs who pull, for puppies still learning, for small and flat-faced breeds, and for any dog with neck, back or breathing issues.

Look for a harness with a **Y-shaped front** that sits on the breastbone and leaves the shoulders free to move. Straps that cross straight over the shoulders can restrict your dog's natural stride.

## Getting the fit right

Fit matters more than any feature.

- **Collar:** you should be able to slide two fingers flat under it. Too loose and it slips over the head; too tight and it rubs.
- **Harness:** two fingers under every strap, no rubbing behind the front legs, and it shouldn't twist when your dog moves.
- **Puppies grow fast.** Check the fit every week or two, and don't buy for the size they'll be in six months.

Check for red or bald patches after the first few walks. They're a sign that something needs adjusting.

## Choosing a lead

For everyday walking, a standard lead of around 1.2 to 2 metres gives you control without tangling. Choose one with a comfortable handle and a sturdy clip that's easy to open with cold fingers.

Visibility matters on dark mornings and winter evenings. A lead with reflective stitching, like our Reflective Walking Leash, catches car headlights and makes you both easier to see. A clip-on light for the collar is a cheap, useful extra.

A few words on other leads:

- **Long lines** (5 to 10 metres) are great for recall training and sniffing in open spaces, but use them with a harness, never a collar, and keep them away from roads.
- **Retractable leads** are best avoided near traffic and in busy places. The thin cord can cause burns and they teach dogs that pulling earns more lead.

## ID and the little extras

In many countries, including much of Europe, dogs must be microchipped, and many places also require an ID tag with your contact details when your dog is in public. Keep the microchip registration up to date whenever you move or change your phone number.

For the walk bag:

- Poop bags, and a clip to carry them on the lead
- A few small, soft treats for rewarding good walking
- Water and a collapsible bowl on longer or warm walks
- A towel in the car for muddy days

## Teaching a loose lead

Gear helps, but a relaxed lead comes from practice. Reward your dog generously when they walk next to you with the lead loose. If they pull, stop and wait until the lead slackens, then carry on. It's slow at first and gets better quickly. Short, frequent training walks work better than one long battle.

Remember that a walk isn't only exercise. Letting your dog stop and sniff is mental work that tires them out in a good way.

## Look after your gear (and paws)

Check collars, harnesses and leads regularly for fraying, cracked clips or loose stitching, and replace anything worn. Most webbing can be hand-washed and dried flat.

In summer, test the pavement with the back of your hand: if you can't hold it there for five seconds, it's too hot for paws. In winter, rinse off road grit and salt after walks. Any limping, sore pads or reluctance to walk is worth a quick check with your vet.

## Quick checklist

- A flat collar with an ID tag, plus a well-fitted harness
- Two fingers under every strap, rechecked as puppies grow
- A 1.2 to 2 metre lead, reflective for dark days
- Microchip details up to date
- Poop bags, treats and water in the walk bag
- Regular checks of gear and paws
