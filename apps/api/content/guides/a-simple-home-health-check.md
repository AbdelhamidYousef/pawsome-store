You know your pet better than anyone. A short, relaxed check once a week turns that instinct into a habit: you learn what "normal" looks like for your dog, cat or rabbit, so you notice quickly when something changes. It takes about five minutes, works for most furry pets, and doubles as a cuddle.

## Pick a calm moment

Choose a time when your pet is already relaxed, such as after a walk or an evening meal. Sit on the floor rather than looming over them, keep a few treats in reach and talk in a low, easy voice. If your pet wriggles away, stop and try again tomorrow. A check that ends happily is one they will let you repeat.

Work in the same order every time, nose to tail. A routine makes it quicker for you and predictable for them.

## Eyes, ears, nose and mouth

- **Eyes** should be bright and clear, with no redness, cloudiness or thick discharge. A little sleep in the corner is normal; constant weeping or squinting is not.
- **Ears** should look clean and smell of nothing much. Dark wax, a sour smell, redness or lots of head shaking are worth a call to your vet.
- **Nose**: wet or dry changes through the day, so do not read too much into it. Look instead for crusting, bleeding or a runny discharge.
- **Mouth**: lift the lip gently. Gums should be pink and moist. Note any very red gum line, broken teeth or breath that is suddenly much worse than usual.

## Coat, skin and body

Run your hands slowly over the whole body, including the belly, armpits and tail. You are feeling for anything new: lumps, scabs, warm or sore patches, or areas where your pet flinches.

Part the fur in a few places to look at the skin. It should be clean and free of flakes, redness or tiny dark specks, which can be a sign of fleas. A glossy coat usually means things are going well; sudden dullness or bald patches deserve attention.

While your hands are there, check body shape. You should be able to feel the ribs under a thin layer of padding without pressing hard, and see a waist from above. Gradual weight changes are easy to miss when you see your pet every day, so a monthly weigh-in on the same scales is a useful extra.

## Paws, nails and bottom

Look between the toes for grass seeds, cuts or redness, and check the pads for cracks. Nails should not click loudly on hard floors or curl towards the pad. Dogs who walk mostly on grass, older cats and indoor rabbits often need trims more often than you would think.

Finally, a quick look under the tail. The area should be clean. Repeated scooting, licking or a dirty bottom can point to anal gland or tummy trouble, and in rabbits often to diet.

## Appetite, thirst and energy

Not everything shows on the outside. Over the week, keep half an eye on:

- **Eating**: is your pet finishing meals at the usual pace? Skipping food for more than a day is worth a call to your vet, and for rabbits and guinea pigs even a few hours without eating is urgent.
- **Drinking**: a big change either way matters. A bowl or a dispenser with a clear tank, like our Gravity Water Dispenser, makes it easy to see how much is going down each day.
- **Toilet habits**: changes in amount, colour or frequency, or straining.
- **Energy and mood**: hiding more, sleeping much more, reluctance to jump or climb stairs, or new grumpiness when touched.

## Keep a simple record

A note on your phone is enough: the date, weight if you have it, and anything new. A photo of a lump next to a coin helps you see whether it is growing. When you do visit the vet, a few weeks of notes are far more useful than "I think he has been a bit quiet lately".

## Quick checklist

- Eyes bright, ears clean, gums pink
- No new lumps, scabs or sore spots
- Skin clean, coat glossy, no flea dirt
- Ribs easy to feel, waist visible
- Paws, pads and nails in good shape
- Eating, drinking and toileting as usual
- Energy and mood as usual

## When to call the vet

This check helps you notice changes. It does not replace a professional exam, and it is not a way to diagnose anything at home. If something looks wrong, or your gut tells you your pet is not themselves, call your vet. Go straight away for breathing trouble, collapse, repeated vomiting, a swollen tummy, or a small pet that has stopped eating. Most vets would much rather see a pet early than late.
