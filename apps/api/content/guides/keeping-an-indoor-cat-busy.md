Indoor cats live longer, safer lives, but they still carry the instincts of a hunter. A cat with nothing to stalk, chase or explore tends to find its own entertainment: the curtains, your ankles at 4 a.m., or simply sleeping and snacking a little too much. The good news is that keeping an indoor cat happy takes less time than most people think. A few short sessions a day and a home set up with cats in mind make all the difference.

## Play like a hunt, not like a chore

Cats don't play for the sake of it. Play is practice for hunting, and the best sessions follow the same pattern: **stalk, chase, pounce, catch, eat**. You can copy that rhythm in ten minutes.

- Move the toy away from your cat, never towards it. Prey runs and hides; it doesn't charge the predator.
- Let the toy disappear behind a chair or under a blanket, then peek out again. The pause before the pounce is half the fun.
- Mix fast dashes with slow, twitchy movements on the floor.
- Let your cat catch the toy often enough to stay interested, and always let them win the last round.

A wand toy such as our Feather Wand Teaser is ideal for this, because it keeps your hand well away from claws and lets you make the "prey" fly, flutter and land. Put wand toys away after each session: they are special, and a dangling string left out is a chewing risk.

## Little and often beats one long session

Most cats are sprinters. Two or three sessions of 5 to 15 minutes each suit them far better than one long workout. Good moments are early morning, early evening and just before your own bedtime, when many cats get their burst of energy anyway.

End each session with a small meal or a few treats. Hunt, catch, eat, groom, sleep: that is the natural cycle, and finishing it helps a lot of cats settle for the night instead of racing around the hallway.

## Toys they can enjoy on their own

You can't play all day, so leave out a few toys your cat can bat around solo. Light toys that skitter across a hard floor work well, as do soft kickers your cat can grab with the front paws and bunny-kick with the back legs. Our Crinkle Mouse Trio and Catnip Banana Kicker were made for exactly that.

A few tips to keep solo toys exciting:

- **Rotate.** Keep most toys in a drawer and put out two or three at a time. Swap them every few days and old favourites feel new again.
- **Refresh the scent.** Storing toys in a tub with a pinch of dried catnip or silvervine makes them more tempting. Not every cat reacts to catnip; around a third simply don't, and that's fine.
- **Check them regularly.** Throw away anything with loose threads, small parts or torn seams.

## Make food a puzzle

In the wild a cat eats many small meals and works for each one. Puzzle feeders, treat balls and snuffle mats bring a bit of that effort back. Start with an easy puzzle so your cat succeeds quickly, then make it harder over time. You can also hide a few dry pieces around the room for a short "treasure hunt". Count any treats as part of the daily food allowance, so playtime doesn't quietly add weight.

## Think vertically and give them a view

Cats experience a room in three dimensions. Shelves, a sturdy cat tree or a cleared windowsill add territory without adding square metres. A perch by a window is often the best entertainment in the house: birds, people and passing leaves are free television. A bird feeder outside the window, where it is safe for the birds, makes the show even better.

Give your cat places to hide as well as places to climb. A cardboard box, a covered bed or a gap under the sofa lets a cat watch the room without being seen, which many cats find deeply reassuring.

## Signs your cat needs more to do

Boredom can look like misbehaviour. Watch for:

- Night-time zoomies, yowling or waking you up for attention
- Scratching furniture or chewing plants and cables
- Over-grooming, or a cat that sleeps almost all day and shows little interest in anything
- Slowly creeping weight gain

These signs can also have a medical cause. Over-grooming, a sudden change in activity or appetite, or any change in litter habits is worth a visit to your vet before you put it down to boredom.

## Quick checklist

- Two or three short play sessions a day, ending with food
- Wand toys put away after use, solo toys rotated every few days
- At least one puzzle feeder or food hunt
- A window perch, something to climb and somewhere to hide
- A vet check if behaviour or appetite changes suddenly
