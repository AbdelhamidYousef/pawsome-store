Every dog has a play style. Some live for the chase, some want a tug-of-war partner, some just want something to carry around and squeak at 6 a.m. Pick a toy that matches the way your dog likes to play and it gets used every day instead of gathering dust under the sofa. Here is how we choose.

## Start with your dog's play style

Watch your dog for a few days and notice what they reach for naturally.

- **The chaser** sprints after anything that moves. Balls and throwable toys are their favourite thing in the world.
- **The tugger** grabs a sleeve, a lead or a towel and pulls. Rope toys and long, sturdy tug toys give that energy a proper outlet.
- **The chewer** settles down with something to gnaw for a long time. They need tougher materials and closer supervision.
- **The carrier** trots around proudly with a toy in their mouth, often soft ones, and may sleep next to them too.

Most dogs are a mix, so a small rotation of two or three types usually covers everything.

## Get the size right

Size matters more than anything else for safety. A toy should be **too big to swallow** and too big to get stuck at the back of the mouth: as a rule of thumb, a ball should be wider than your dog's open jaw, not just their mouth at rest. Big dogs need big toys, even if a small one looks cuter. Puppies and very small breeds, on the other hand, get frustrated with toys they cannot carry comfortably.

When your dog grows or you add a bigger dog to the household, re-check the whole toy box.

## Match the material to the job

- **Rope** is great for tug and gentle chewing, and the fibres give teeth a light clean while they play. Our Braided Tug Rope has a knot at each end so both of you get a good grip. Bin it once it starts to unravel into long strands, which should not be swallowed.
- **Rubber** suits fetch and chewing. Look for natural or food-safe rubber with some give: a toy that is harder than your dog's teeth can damage them. A bouncy ball with an unpredictable bounce, like our Bouncy Rubber Ball, makes fetch far more exciting.
- **Plush** is for cuddling, carrying and gentle play. Squeakers make soft toys irresistible, so pick ones with reinforced seams, and take them away if your dog starts surgery on the stuffing.

## Play together, not just alone

Toys work best as part of a game with you. Tug teaches impulse control when you add a simple "drop" cue and reward it with another round. Fetch burns energy fast and builds a great recall. Short sessions of five to ten minutes, a few times a day, are better than one long marathon, especially for puppies and older dogs with stiff joints.

Keep one or two **special toys** that only come out for play with you. They stay exciting, which makes them a brilliant reward during training.

## Rotate, inspect and replace

Dogs get bored of the same toys. Keep three or four out at a time and swap the rest in every week or so: an "old" toy back from the cupboard often feels brand new.

When you swap, give each toy a quick inspection. Throw away anything with cracks, loose pieces, torn seams or missing chunks. Wash rope and plush toys regularly (most go in the washing machine) and rinse rubber toys in warm, soapy water.

## Quick checklist

- Toy matches your dog's play style
- Too big to swallow, with room to spare
- Material suits the game: rope to tug, rubber to fetch and chew, plush to cuddle
- Two or three types in rotation, swapped weekly
- Damaged toys go in the bin, not back in the box
- Unsupervised time only with toys your dog cannot destroy

## A word on safety

No toy is truly indestructible, and every dog chews differently. Supervise new toys until you know how your dog treats them. If your dog swallows a piece of toy, or shows signs like vomiting, gagging, a tender tummy or straining to go to the toilet, call your vet straight away rather than waiting to see.
