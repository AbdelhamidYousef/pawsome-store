Cats sleep for up to 16 hours a day, and where they sleep says a lot about how safe they feel. In the wild a cat is both hunter and hunted, so the perfect resting place lets them stay hidden or keep watch, ideally both. Give your cat a few well-chosen spots and you will see more stretched-out naps, less stress and fewer claims on your laptop keyboard.

## Why cats need both a hideout and a lookout

Cats relax when they feel in control of their surroundings. A **hideout** is enclosed on most sides, so nothing can surprise them from behind. A **lookout** is high up, so they can see what is coming long before it arrives. Shy cats, new arrivals and cats in busy homes lean on hideouts; confident cats and keen bird-watchers love a lookout. Most cats use both, depending on their mood and the time of day.

## Making a great hideout

- **Enclosed, with one clear entrance.** A covered bed works well; our Cosy Cave Bed has a soft, deep cushion inside and a domed roof that keeps the warmth in.
- **Out of the traffic.** A quiet corner, the space beside the sofa or under a desk beats the middle of the hallway.
- **Away from food and litter.** Cats do not like to eat, sleep and toilet in the same place. Keep beds a good few steps from bowls and the tray.
- **Never a trap.** Do not pull a cat out of their hideout or use it for anything unpleasant, like catching them for the carrier. It has to stay a guaranteed safe zone.

A cardboard box with a blanket inside is a perfectly good hideout too. If your cat chooses the box over the bed, take it as useful information, not an insult.

## Making a great lookout

Height means safety and entertainment. Window sills, the top of a bookcase or a shelf on the wall all count, as long as the spot is stable and easy to reach. A cushion that fixes to the window, like our Window Perch Cushion, turns a narrow sill into a sunny observation deck with a view of birds, leaves and passing dogs.

Make the climb easy. Older cats and kittens appreciate a "staircase" of a chair, then a low shelf, then the perch. If you have several cats, give them more than one high spot so nobody has to queue or fight for the best view.

## Warmth, texture and the seasons

Cats love warmth: it is why they follow patches of sun around the house. In winter, move a bed near (not on) a radiator, add a fleece blanket, or place a bed where the afternoon sun lands. In summer, they may prefer a cool tiled floor or a shaded spot, so leave those options open rather than shutting doors.

Texture matters too. Some cats love deep, fluffy fabric; others prefer a flatter, firmer surface they can knead. If a new bed is ignored, swap the cover before you give up on it.

## Helping a cat accept a new bed

Cats are suspicious of new things that smell of the shop. Make the introduction easy:

1. Put the bed where your cat already likes to sleep, rather than where it looks best.
2. Add something that smells of home, such as an old T-shirt or their usual blanket.
3. Scatter a few treats inside or play with a wand toy nearby, so the bed becomes part of something nice.
4. Give it time. Many cats take a week to move in, and then refuse to leave.

## Quick checklist

- At least one enclosed hideout, in a quiet corner
- At least one high, stable lookout with a view
- Beds away from food bowls and the litter tray
- A warm spot for winter and a cool one for summer
- One resting place per cat, plus one extra
- New beds placed where your cat already naps

## When to look closer

A change in where or how much your cat sleeps can be a clue. If your cat suddenly hides much more than usual, stops jumping up to their favourite high spot, or seems restless and unable to settle, it may be pain or illness rather than a change of taste. In that case, have a chat with your vet.
