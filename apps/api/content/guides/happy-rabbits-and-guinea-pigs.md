Rabbits and guinea pigs are curious, chatty and full of character, but they are also prey animals. Everything about how they live is shaped by staying safe: they eat little and often, they hide when worried, and they hide illness too. Get the basics right and you will see the best of them: binkies, popcorning, purring and contented munching.

## Hay is the main meal, not a side

The single most important thing you can give a rabbit or guinea pig is **unlimited fresh hay**. It should make up most of what they eat, every day. Hay keeps their gut moving and, just as important, wears down their teeth, which never stop growing. A good rule: a pile of hay at least as big as your pet, refreshed daily.

Look for hay that is green, sweet-smelling and dry, never dusty or mouldy. Long strands like our Meadow Hay Bundle encourage proper chewing. Put hay in more than one place, including next to the litter area, as both species like to eat while they toilet.

## Fresh greens and a little pellet

Alongside hay, offer a daily portion of fresh leafy greens and herbs, such as romaine lettuce, coriander, parsley or dandelion leaves. Introduce new greens one at a time, in small amounts, so tummies can adjust. Fruit and carrots are treats, not staples: they are high in sugar.

A small measure of plain, high-fibre pellets fills any gaps. Avoid muesli-style mixes, which let pets pick out the sweet bits and leave the rest.

**Guinea pigs need vitamin C every day**, as they cannot make their own. Peppers, kale and parsley are good sources, and your vet can advise on supplements.

## Somewhere to hide

A rabbit or guinea pig without a hiding place lives in a state of low-level stress. Give every animal at least one hideout of their own, with two exits if possible so nobody gets cornered by a companion. A wooden house like our Cosy Hideaway House doubles as a lookout platform for rabbits who like to sit on top and survey their kingdom.

Tunnels are popular too: they give a safe route across open floor, and guinea pigs in particular love to zoom through them.

## Room to move and things to chew

Both species need far more space than a traditional hutch or cage offers. Rabbits should be able to hop several times in a row, stand fully upright and stretch out flat. Guinea pigs need room to run laps, which they will do at surprising speed when happy. A large pen with a secure shelter, or a rabbit-proofed room, is ideal.

Give them safe things to chew and explore: untreated wood, willow, cardboard boxes and woven hay toys. Our Tunnel Chew Roll does both jobs, as a hideout to run through and something to nibble. Rotate toys weekly to keep curious minds busy.

## Company matters

Rabbits and guinea pigs are social animals and are happiest with at least one friend of their own kind. Neutered pairs or small groups work best. Introduce new companions slowly and on neutral ground, and ask your vet or a rescue for advice before bonding. Rabbits and guinea pigs should not be housed together: they communicate differently and have different dietary needs.

## Quick checklist

- Unlimited fresh hay, topped up every day
- Leafy greens daily, fruit only as a treat
- Vitamin C for guinea pigs
- Fresh water in a bowl or bottle, checked daily
- A hideout per animal, ideally with two exits
- Space to run, stretch and stand up
- Safe chews and toys, rotated weekly
- At least one companion of the same species

## Signs to call the vet

Small pets hide illness well, so small changes matter. **A rabbit or guinea pig that stops eating or stops passing droppings needs a vet the same day**, as their gut can stop working quickly. Also call if you notice drooling, weight loss, runny eyes or nose, a dirty bottom, overgrown teeth or nails, or a usually lively pet sitting hunched and still. A vet who regularly sees rabbits and guinea pigs is well worth finding before you need one.
