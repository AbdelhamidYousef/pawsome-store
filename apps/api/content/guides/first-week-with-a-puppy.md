Bringing a puppy home is pure joy, with a side of chaos. Your puppy has just left their mum, their littermates and everything they know, so the first week is less about teaching rules and more about helping them feel safe. Get the routine right now and house training, sleep and good manners all come more easily later.

## Before they arrive

A little preparation saves a lot of stress on day one.

- **Puppy-proof one room first.** Lift cables, shoes, houseplants and anything chewable off the floor. It is easier to give a puppy one safe room than to watch a whole house.
- **Set up a den.** Choose a quiet corner, not a busy hallway, with a bed, a water bowl and a soft toy. A comfortable, washable bed with raised sides, like our Cloud Comfort Bed, gives them something to snuggle against.
- **Ask for a scent.** If you can, take a blanket to the breeder or shelter a few days early and bring it home with your puppy. Familiar smells are deeply comforting.
- **Stock the basics:** the same food they are already eating, a collar and lead, poo bags, an enzyme cleaner for accidents and a few chew toys.

## Day one: keep it quiet

It is tempting to invite everyone round to meet the newest family member. Wait. For the first few days, keep visitors to a minimum and let your puppy explore at their own pace. Show them their den, their water and their toilet spot, then let them sleep, which they will do a lot: 18 to 20 hours a day is normal.

Children should sit on the floor and let the puppy come to them, rather than picking them up or chasing them.

## Build a simple routine

Puppies feel safe when life is predictable. Aim for the same rhythm every day:

1. Wake up, then straight outside to the toilet spot.
2. Breakfast, then outside again.
3. A short play or training session, then a nap.
4. Repeat through the day: toilet, play, sleep, with meals at regular times.
5. A calm last toilet trip before bed.

Write it on the fridge so everyone in the house follows the same plan.

## House training basics

Take your puppy outside **after every sleep, meal and play session**, and at least every hour or two while they are awake. Stand quietly in the same spot and wait. When they go, praise them warmly and give a tiny reward right away, within a couple of seconds.

Accidents will happen. Never scold or rub their nose in it: it only teaches them to hide when they need to go. Clean up with an enzyme cleaner so the smell does not draw them back, and take them out a little more often.

## Sleep and the first nights

The first nights can be noisy. Many puppies settle better if their bed is close to yours at first, so they can hear and smell you. You can move it gradually to its permanent place over the following weeks. Expect a toilet trip in the middle of the night, keep it boring (no play, dim lights), then straight back to bed.

## Gentle training starts now

Training in week one is about building a bond, not perfect obedience. Practise their name, "sit" and coming to you, in sessions of two or three minutes. Use small, soft rewards your puppy can eat in a second; our Soft Training Bites can be broken into even smaller pieces for tiny mouths. Count treats as part of the daily food allowance so their tummy stays happy.

Socialisation also starts now, gently. Let your puppy see, hear and sniff new things from a safe distance: the hoover, the doorbell, people in hats. Calm, short and positive is the goal. Until your vet says vaccinations are complete, carry them outside rather than letting them walk where unknown dogs go.

## Quick checklist

- One puppy-proofed room and a quiet den
- The same food they ate before, for now
- Toilet trips after every sleep, meal and game
- Praise and a tiny treat the moment they get it right
- A written daily routine everyone follows
- Short, fun training: name, sit, come
- Visitors and outings kept calm and brief

## Your first vet visit

Book a check-up in the first week. Your vet will examine your puppy, talk you through vaccinations, worming, flea treatment and microchipping, and answer questions about food and growth. Call sooner if your puppy is lethargic, stops eating, has diarrhoea or vomits more than once: young puppies can go downhill quickly, so it is always better to check.
